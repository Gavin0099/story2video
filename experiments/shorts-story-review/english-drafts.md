# First twelve Shorts · English story drafts

Each entry includes the reveal. These drafts preserve the clues in the Traditional Chinese cards. Read time and subtitle boundaries are checked during production.

## 01 · He Put the Poster Back

After his wife's funeral, a station attendant handed him a missing-person poster. “Do you want to take this with you?” He recognized his wife's yellow raincoat in a photo from the flooded platform.

At the trash can, he read the first line. “If you remember reaching out at the edge of the platform that day…” It was signed by his wife six months earlier. He put it back up and replaced her number with his own.

Why put up the poster again?

Someone had pulled her onto the platform. She'd used the photo so that person could recognize her. Now he could take the call for her. He wrote two words beside his number. “Thank you.”

## 02 · The Light in the Window

As a rescue boat came down the street, a boy stepped down toward the water and waved his flashlight. His sister pulled him behind a low wall. “Across the street,” she said.

Only two steps of the flooded stairwell were above water. The opening faced the shops. He'd waved at the passing boat before. It never stopped. This time she steadied his wrist and he clicked twice. A shop window angled toward the street flashed twice. The boat turned back.

Why aim across the street?

The side wall hid them from the boat. The angled glass reflected their signal where the driver could see it. He turned to check the opening. When a rescuer reached down, she pushed her brother forward. “Take him first.”

## 03 · She Sewed the Buttonholes Shut

The tailor sewed every buttonhole shut. But her customer had asked her to keep every original button.

His right hand was in a cast. He tried to button the jacket left-handed, but the fabric slipped away. He needed that uniform at the front desk the next day. She stitched the old buttons over the closed holes, then pressed two scraps of fabric together. Click.

Why sew the buttonholes shut?

She'd put snap fasteners inside the jacket. The old buttons covered the stitches. He opened his mouth to ask for help. Before he spoke, the first snap clicked shut under his left hand. He did the rest himself.

## 04 · He Muted His Mother's Lines

After the community theater rehearsal, he muted every line his mother had spoken. He left in another actor's cough.

Whenever she lost her place, her scene partner finished the line for her. Afterward she said, “I'd like to get through that part myself.” At home he played the recording. Her partner called her character's name. A few seconds of silence followed, exactly as long as her line. Then the next actor spoke.

Why leave the silence?

It was a rehearsal track. Every cue was still there, with time for her to speak. That evening she heard her character's name, paused, and finished the line. He let her finish.

## 05 · Don't Look for Page Six

At a small theater, a volunteer called out, “There's only the folded cover sheet. Where's page three?” The stage manager didn't look up. “Page six will be gone too. Give them another copy.”

She only had a blank mockup. The eight-page program was made from two sheets, each folded once, one inside the other. The volunteer opened the copy flat. The front and back covers were still joined, with no torn edges.

How did she know which other page was missing?

Pages one, two, seven, and eight are on the outer sheet. Three through six are on the inner sheet. Lose the inner sheet and you lose both three and six. The volunteer checked the stack and found two more copies with only the outer sheet.

## 06 · The Price List at the Back of the Line

The print shop owner took the price list down from the counter and put it at the entrance to the line. He even turned it so customers at the counter couldn't read it.

At lunch, the copier sat idle half the time while the line reached the door. The sign had hung low. Only when it was their turn could customers see the paper sizes and binding options. “Hang on. I haven't decided.”

Why move the sign and turn it around?

Facing the line entrance, the list gave people time to choose while they waited. The next customer walked up with the file ready. “A4, double-sided, one staple in the corner.”

## 07 · She Went to the Back of the Line

On her daughter's first day cooking alone at the family food cart, she came by. Her daughter reached for the apron she was carrying. She put it away and joined the line.

Yesterday, she'd taken the spatula halfway through a practice run. “When you do that,” her daughter said, “I never get to find out how mine would taste.” This time she ordered the new soup her daughter had written on the menu and counted out the exact price.

Why line up at her own cart?

Today she was a customer. She was letting her daughter finish the whole pot her way. After her first spoonful, her daughter asked, “Should I change anything?” She held the bowl steady. “Will you have it again tomorrow?”

## 08 · The First Test Print

A gust scattered three sheets from a printmaking class. The teacher turned them over and knew which had been printed first and which had been printed last.

Each front had an arrow in red, blue, or yellow. Every new sheet went face up onto the stack while the ink was still wet. The red print had a clean back. The blue print had faint red ink on its back. The yellow print had faint blue.

How could she put them in order?

Each sheet picked up ink from the one below it. Red marked the back of blue. Blue marked the back of yellow. The stack grew upward, so the order was red, blue, yellow. She restacked them and showed the class how the marks lined up.

## 09 · The Door Almost Closed

A bakery had orders for three tall cakes. Before icing any of them, the owner put three empty boxes in the fridge. The apprentice pointed to the wider shelf. The owner moved them to the lower one.

There was room on either side of the boxes upstairs, but the rack inside the door bumped a front corner when the owner tried to close it. On the lower shelf, the boxes could be staggered and pushed farther back.

Why put empty boxes in the fridge before the cakes were ready?

He was testing the space the finished boxes would need with the door closed. Once the first cake was boxed, the apprentice used the spot they'd tested. The door closed cleanly.

## 10 · He Kept the Deepest Scratch

He sanded the scratches out of his father's workbench, but taped over the deepest one. His father pushed the sandpaper toward it. “You missed the worst one.”

There was a fleck of blue paint on its edge. He took a wooden toy car out of a drawer. It had one missing wheel and a chip in its blue front. His foot touched the low stool he'd used when he was too small to reach the bench.

Why keep the deepest scratch?

That car was the first thing he'd made. An exposed nail had gouged the bench when it slipped. He'd expected his father to send him away. His father pulled the stool closer, held the car, and let him finish. He peeled back the tape. “I'm keeping this one.”

## 11 · Seat Eight Was Across the Aisle

Three friends bought seats seven, eight, and nine in the same row. The usher sent eight to the right and the other two to the left. Nobody had exchanged tickets.

All three tickets were for row six of the same show. The two seats nearest the aisle on the left were one and three. On the right they were two and four. Seat five was on the left. Six was on the right.

Why weren't seven, eight, and nine together?

Each side starts numbering at the aisle. The left takes the odd numbers and the right takes the even ones. Seven and nine sit side by side. Eight is across the aisle. Once they sat down, seven waved hello to eight.

## 12 · A Toy Car on the Table

For the third time, customers at the window table complained that a wet-bottomed glass had slid toward the window without anyone touching the table. A barista brought over a toy car with no motor.

He dried the table and set the car down in the middle, facing the door. It rolled backward toward the window. He caught it and put it back in the same spot, facing the other way. It rolled toward the window again.

Why turn the car around?

He wanted to see whether it followed its own orientation or the slope of the table. Both times it rolled toward the same edge. He raised the window-side leg a little and tried again. The car stayed put. So did the next customer's glass.
